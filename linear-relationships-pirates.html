Charting the Linear Seas

Three charts stand between you and your Navigator's badge: Linear Relationships, Finding the Rule, and Reading the Line on a Graph. Chart your course, Cap'n.

⚓

Why chart a straight line at all? Because a huge slice of the real world moves at a constant rate — a ship's steady speed, a phone plan's per-minute cost, a savings account earning the same amount each week. Once you can spot the rule behind a straight line, you can predict the future from just a few data points — that's the whole game of mathematical modelling.

CHART I

Linear Relationships

A linear relationship is what you get when two things change together at a constant rate — every equal step forward produces the exact same-size jump. Plot it, and you always get a dead-straight line.

Steady Wind, Straight Line

Imagine a ship sailing with a steady wind in its sails. Every hour, it covers exactly the same distance. Here's the ship's log:

Time, x (hours)0123
Distance, y (km)271217

Every hour, the distance jumps by exactly +5 km. That constant jump is what makes this relationship linear — and it's exactly what a straight line looks like on a graph.

OLD SEA DOG'S TIP A steady wind gives a straight line. A gusty, unpredictable wind gives you a wobbly, curved path — not linear at all. If the jump between steps isn't always the same size, it isn't a linear relationship.

The Form of Every Linear Rule

Every linear relationship can be written as:

y = mx + c
  • m is the gradient — the constant rate of change (how much y jumps per step of x). In the ship's log, m = 5.
  • c is the y-intercept — the starting value, i.e. the value of y when x = 0. In the ship's log, c = 2 (the ship started 2 km from harbour before it even set off).

So the ship's log above is modelled by the rule y = 5x + 2.

CHART II

Finding the Linear Rule

Given a table or two points, you can work backwards to find the rule y = mx + c that produced them — like a navigator reading a log to figure out the ship's speed and starting point.

Two Steps to Any Rule

Step 1 — Find the gradient (m): pick two points and divide the change in y by the change in x:

m = (y₂ − y₁) ÷ (x₂ − x₁)   (also called "rise over run")

Step 2 — Find the intercept (c): substitute one point into y = mx + c and solve for c. If one of your points already has x = 0, c is simply that y-value.

OLD SEA DOG'S TIP Think of it like reading a treasure log. The gradient is "treasure found per day" — how fast the pile grows. The intercept is "treasure the crew already had in the chest before counting started." Find the daily rate first, then work out what they started with.

Worked Example — From a Table

Table: x = 0,1,2,3 → y = 2,5,8,11. Find the rule.
STEP 1 Gradient: each step of x adds 3 to y, so m = 3
STEP 2 At x = 0, y = 2, so c = 2
ANSWER y = 3x + 2

Worked Example — From Two Points

Points (1, 4) and (3, 10). Find the rule.
STEP 1 m = (10 − 4) ÷ (3 − 1) = 6 ÷ 2 = 3
STEP 2 Substitute (1, 4): 4 = 3(1) + c → c = 1
ANSWER y = 3x + 1

Worked Example — Negative Gradient

Points (0, 8) and (4, 0). Find the rule.
STEP 1 m = (0 − 8) ÷ (4 − 0) = −8 ÷ 4 = −2
STEP 2 One point already has x = 0, so c = 8 directly
ANSWER y = −2x + 8

CHART III

Reading the Line on a Graph

Every part of y = mx + c has a job on the graph. Play with the sliders below and watch the line move — you're the captain now.

What m and c Actually Do

  • Gradient (m) is the steepness and direction. Positive m → the line sails uphill left to right. Negative m → it sails downhill. The bigger the number (ignoring the sign), the steeper the climb or drop. m = 0 is dead calm — a flat, horizontal line.
  • Y-intercept (c) is the ship's home port — the exact spot where the line crosses the y-axis, when x = 0.
OLD SEA DOG'S TIP Picture the y-axis as the harbour wall. c tells you exactly where on that wall your voyage begins. m tells you which way you sail from there, and how steeply you climb or dive as you go.
